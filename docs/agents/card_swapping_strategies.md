# Wingspan Strategy: Mathematical Modeling of Card-Swapping and Hand-Cycling Powers

In Wingspan, brown powers that cycle cards—such as **"Draw 2, Discard 1"** or **"Tuck 1 from hand, Draw 1"**—serve as vital variance-reduction and resource-conversion engines. They do not just generate raw points; they optimize the quality and point-density of your hand per turn spent. 

This guide outlines the formal decision-making model, the utility equations, and a practical heuristic to determine when to snatch a card from the tray versus risking a blind deck draw.

---

## 1. The Core Metric: Net Point Value ($V$)

To evaluate any card-cycling action, every bird card must be quantified into a single metric: **Net Point Value ($V$)**. A card’s true value is the sum of its raw scoring potential, engine-building capabilities, and synergy with objectives, minus its setup costs:

$$V = P_{\text{raw}} + V_{\text{engine}} + V_{\text{bonus}} + V_{\text{goal}} - C_{\text{food}} - C_{\text{egg}}$$

### Parameter Breakdown:
*   **$P_{\text{raw}}$**: The printed victory points on the card.
*   **$V_{\text{engine}}$**: The expected points this bird will generate over the remainder of the game via its power. (e.g., an egg-laying bird played in Round 1 has high $V_{\text{engine}}$; played in Round 4, it approaches $0$).
*   **$V_{\text{bonus}}$ / $V_{\text{goal}}$**: The probability-weighted point contribution toward your active Bonus Cards and End-of-Round goals.
*   **$C_{\text{food}}$ / $C_{\text{egg}}$**: The opportunity cost of the food tokens and eggs required to play the bird (conventionally valued at roughly $1$ VP per resource spent).

---

## 2. Modeling the Brown Powers

Activating a card-swapping power is mathematically equivalent to executing a financial option to upgrade an asset in your hand.

### A. Tuck 1 from Hand, Draw 1 (Tray or Deck)
This power guarantees a baseline of **$+1 \text{ VP}$** (from the tucked card) while allowing you to cycle a low-value card. 

When pulling a known card from the **Tray**, you trade a known asset in your hand ($V_{\text{hand}}$) for a known asset in the tray ($V_{\text{tray}}$). The net value change ($\Delta V$) of this specific action is modeled as:

$$\Delta V = 1 + V_{\text{tray}} - V_{\text{hand}}$$

*   **The Decision Threshold:** You should *always* activate this power if $\Delta V > 1$. Even if the tray card is identical in value to your hand card ($V_{\text{tray}} = V_{\text{hand}}$), you net $+1 \text{ VP}$ from the physical act of tucking.

### B. Draw 2, Discard 1 (Blind Deck)
When drawing blind from the deck, you sample from a random distribution. Let $\mathbb{E}[V_{\text{deck}}]$ be the average value of a random card in the deck. 

Drawing $2$ cards and keeping $1$ means your output is the maximum value of two independent random variables: $\max(V_1, V_2)$. Statistically, the expected value of the *maximum* of two draws is significantly higher than a single draw, drastically flattening your variance curve. You then discard your worst card ($V_{\text{worst}}$).

$$\Delta V = \mathbb{E}[\max(V_1, V_2)] - V_{\text{worst}}$$

---

## 3. The Tray Heuristic: "To Snatch or to Risk?"

When a highly synergistic card appears in the open tray ($V_{\text{tray}}$), you must decide whether to use your brown power to grab it immediately, or execute a different action (like gathering food) and risk leaving it in the tray for a turn.

We can model this using a **Decision Tree under Risk**. Let $p$ be the probability that an opponent steals the card before your next turn, and $A_{\text{setup}}$ be the value generated by performing an alternate setup action this turn.

*   **Option A (Grab Now):** You secure the card immediately. Your immediate hand value increases by $V_{\text{tray}}$.
*   **Option B (Risk It / Wait):** You delay the grab. 
    *   If the card survives (probability $1-p$), you get $V_{\text{tray}}$ next turn plus this turn's $A_{\text{setup}}$.
    *   If the card is stolen (probability $p$), you are forced to blind-draw from the deck next turn ($\mathbb{E}[V_{\text{deck}}]$).

### The Tray Snatch Threshold Formula
You should **grab the card from the tray immediately** using your brown power if:

$$V_{\text{tray}} - V_{\text{hand}} > (1 - p)(V_{\text{tray}} + A_{\text{setup}}) + p(\mathbb{E}[V_{\text{deck}}]) - A_{\text{setup}}$$

By simplifying and isolating $V_{\text{tray}}$, we establish the formal **Tray Snatch Heuristic**:

$$V_{\text{tray}} > \mathbb{E}[V_{\text{deck}}] + \frac{V_{\text{hand}} - p \cdot A_{\text{setup}}}{p}$$

---

## 4. Strategic Rules of Thumb for Tabletop Play

Because computing exact algebraic equations mid-turn is impractical, players can rely on three structural rules derived directly from the mathematical model:

1.  **The Competitor Scale ($p$-Modifier):** In a 2-player game (or Duet Mode), $p$ is naturally low unless the tray card matches your opponent's visible goal path. In a 4 or 5-player game, $p$ approaches $1.0$ for any card with a printed value $>5$. Scale your grabbing urgency strictly by player count.
2.  **The "Trash to Treasure" Delta ($V_{\text{tray}} - V_{\text{hand}}$):** If you are holding an unplayable, high-cost bird in late Round 3 ($V_{\text{hand}} \approx 0$) and a cheap, end-of-round goal matching bird lands in the tray ($V_{\text{tray}} \approx 6$), the delta is massive. Execute the swap immediately.
3.  **The Deck Decay Rule:** In Round 1, $\mathbb{E}[V_{\text{deck}}]$ is high because almost any bird can anchor an engine. By Round 4, $\mathbb{E}[V_{\text{deck}}]$ drops below zero because drawing a bird you do not have time to play costs an entire tempo turn. Consequently, **the tray becomes infinitely more valuable than the blind deck in the final round.**

# Implementation Review: What the Agents Actually Do

Added 2026-09-04. The model above is sound; none of it is implemented. Below is
what exists, the gaps, and the two places the model needs correcting.

## The heuristic you asked about already exists — and is not wired in

`tray_preference.base_card_affinity(card, player, state)` is close to the
$V$ metric above. It scores victory points, egg limit, affordability (now
probability-weighted against the birdfeeder), habitat room, and power colour, and
`bonus_card_focus_affinity` adds bonus-card fit. `net_value` additionally computes
a bonus-card **posterior over opponents** for denial.

So the machinery to compare $V_{\text{tray}}$ against $V_{\text{hand}}$ is built.
It is used only for `DRAW_CARDS` **actions**. It is not used anywhere in power
resolution.

## Gap 1: card-cycling powers draw from the deck only

`_resolve_draw_cards_then_discard_power` (`base_game.py:1064`) calls
`_draw_cards_from_deck`, which pops from `state.decks.bird_deck` and never looks
at the tray. Every "draw 2, keep 1" power is therefore modelled as a blind draw,
even where the card permits taking from the tray. Section 2B's
$\mathbb{E}[\max(V_1, V_2)]$ is being applied to the wrong distribution, and the
tray-snatch decision in section 3 cannot arise at all.

## Gap 2: the discard choice is a stub

Every discard routes through:

```python
def _choose_discard_card_for_food(player: PlayerState) -> str:
    card = min(player.hand, key=lambda card: (card.victory_points, -card.food_cost.minimum_total))
```

Lowest printed points, tie-broken by highest cost. That is $P_{\text{raw}}$ alone
— it discards on raw points while ignoring $V_{\text{engine}}$, $V_{\text{bonus}}$,
$V_{\text{goal}}$ and playability. It will happily discard a cheap 2-point bird
that completes a bonus card and keep an unaffordable 5-point bird.

The function is named for one purpose and reused for four (`base_game.py:649`,
`:1013`, `:1071`, `:1406`), so fixing it improves every cycling power at once.
It should call the same affinity function used for tray preference.

## Gap 3: "draw the tray" takes everything

`_resolve_draw_tray_cards_power` (`base_game.py:1292`) assigns the entire tray to
the hand. Where a card says "draw N from the tray", no selection happens.

## Correction 1: the tuck threshold in section 2A

The doc states you should always activate if $\Delta V > 1$, then says that even
at $V_{\text{tray}} = V_{\text{hand}}$ you net $+1$ VP. Those are inconsistent: at
equal values $\Delta V = 1$, which does **not** satisfy $\Delta V > 1$. The
intended rule is $\Delta V > 0$, or equivalently
$V_{\text{tray}} > V_{\text{hand}} - 1$.

## Correction 2: the snatch formula omits the action cube

The threshold in section 3 compares card values and $A_{\text{setup}}$ but never
charges for the turn itself. In Wingspan the action cube is the binding
constraint — 8/7/6/5 per round, 26 total. A model that treats "grab now" and
"do something else and grab later" as differing only in risk will over-grab,
because grabbing later is only free if a spare cube exists.

The comparison should be per-cube:

$$\frac{V_{\text{tray}} - V_{\text{hand}}}{1 \text{ cube}} \;\;\text{vs}\;\; \frac{A_{\text{setup}}}{1 \text{ cube}} + \text{(risk-adjusted value of the delayed grab)}$$

This also explains why rule 3 ("Deck Decay") is right for a reason the doc does
not give: in round 4 the deck is not worth less because cards are worse, but
because a drawn card has no cube left to play it with.

## Gap the drafted model does not cover: denial is one-sided

Section 3 treats $p$ — the chance an opponent takes the card — as exogenous. The
agents already estimate the other half: `net_value`'s `_opponent_card_value` uses
`_played_power_value` against the opponent's board plus bonus-card fit posterior
to value denial. $p$ should be derived from that estimate rather than from player
count alone.

Rule 1's "in a 4 or 5-player game, $p$ approaches 1.0 for any card with printed
value > 5" is a reasonable table heuristic but wrong as a model: opponents take
cards that fit *their* board, and a 6-point bird nobody can feed sits in the tray
all game.

## Recommended sequence

1. Replace `_choose_discard_card_for_food` with an affinity-based choice. One
   function, improves four call sites, and is straightforwardly correct.
2. Let deck-draw powers see the tray where the card text allows it.
3. Add selection to the tray-draw power.
4. Derive $p$ from the existing opponent posterior rather than player count.
5. Only then consider the full snatch-threshold model — and ablate it, since the
   last two valuation improvements both measured null.

Items 1-3 are fidelity fixes with a plausible correctness argument. Item 5 is a
strategy term and carries no such presumption.
